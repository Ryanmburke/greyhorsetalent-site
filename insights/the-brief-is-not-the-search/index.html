The brief is not the search.

A strong brief gives the search direction. The market determines whether the assumptions behind it hold up. The best searches use both.

The brief matters. It aligns the company around what it thinks it needs. But until that brief meets the market, some of it is still an assumption.

01 / The starting point

A brief is a direction, not a verdict.

A technical search needs a starting point. The role, team, company stage, technical problem and expected ownership must be clear enough to define where the search should begin.

The mistake is treating that first version of the brief as if it already contains the answer.

Terms like Founding Engineer, Staff Engineer, or VP Engineering are useful shorthand. They organize the conversation, but they do not guarantee that the strongest candidate carries that title today.

A better opening question

Instead of asking, “What title are we hiring?” ask, “What does this person need to own, change, or make true in the first 12 months?”

That answer gives the search something more durable than a title. It creates a mandate that can be tested against real people and real markets.

The brief gives the search direction. The market tells you whether that direction is producing the people you actually need.
02 / Market evidence

The market tests the assumptions behind the brief.

Once the search moves into market mapping and candidate conversations, the quality of the information changes.

You stop asking only what the company wants and start seeing what the relevant talent market is actually showing.

01Who is relevant

Comparable work may sit under different titles, inside adjacent companies, or across technical disciplines that were not obvious in the first brief.

02Who engages

The right profiles may respond differently from the market the company originally expected to target.

03What repeats

Recurring questions and objections can expose gaps around scope, seniority, compensation, team design, or how the opportunity is being explained.

04What gets stronger

Real candidate conversations often make it easier to distinguish essential requirements from preferences that were simply present in the original brief.

Those signals do not automatically mean the brief was wrong. They mean the search now has evidence that did not exist when it was written.

03 / Recalibration

Change the search when the evidence is consistent.

Recalibration does not mean changing direction every time one candidate says no. A search becomes noisy if every conversation rewrites the mandate.

The useful signals are patterns.

The title is consistently too narrow.

Strong candidates are doing comparable work under titles the original search did not include. The requirement may still be right. The market definition is too tight.

The expected companies are producing weaker profiles.

An adjacent technical environment may be creating people with more relevant ownership, stage experience, or problem exposure than the obvious competitor set.

The opportunity is creating the same hesitation.

If strong candidates repeatedly question the same part of the scope, reporting line, compensation, or decision authority, that deserves attention. Pushing harder on outreach does not resolve a structural mismatch.

Real profiles change what “good” looks like.

Seeing credible candidates often helps a team become more precise. Something originally treated as essential may become secondary. A capability that looked optional may prove central.

Calibrate first. Recalibrate when the evidence says to.
04 / The constant

The outcome should stay clearer than the specification.

A responsive search is not an excuse for an undefined search.

The role can evolve. A different title, background, or market may prove more relevant. But the reason for making the hire should stay clear.

If the person still needs to build the first engineering function, stabilize a critical system, lead the next stage of growth, or bridge software and hardware, that outcome becomes the anchor.

The specification can change around better evidence. The outcome keeps the search from drifting.

The practical test

When a new profile changes the conversation, ask whether the change makes the search more likely to deliver the original business outcome, or simply makes the search easier.

Those are not the same thing.

The point

A strong brief should make a technical search more focused. A strong search should also be capable of making the brief more accurate.
